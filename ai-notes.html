What ML observability actually catches

Disclosure: the article below was generated by an AI model (Claude, Anthropic) from a short prompt describing the topic and tone, then reviewed and lightly edited for this page. It is included to satisfy this assignment's requirement for an AI-generated page. Full prompt details are in the project README.

Most teams find out a model has failed the same way they find out a pipe has burst: from the mess, not the gauge. By the time a support ticket mentions "the recommendations look off," the model has usually been wrong for days. Observability exists to move that discovery earlier, and in practice it catches a narrower, more specific set of problems than the word suggests.

Input drift is the quiet one

A model trained on last year's traffic doesn't know that users, prices, or upstream systems have changed. Input drift monitoring compares the statistical shape of incoming features against the training distribution — not to prove the model is wrong, but to flag that the ground it's standing on has shifted. A sudden jump in null rates for one field is often the first sign of an upstream schema change, not a modeling problem at all.

Prediction drift tells a different story

Where input drift watches what goes in, prediction drift watches what comes out: the distribution of scores or labels a model produces over time. A classifier that once flagged two percent of transactions as risky and now flags twelve percent is worth investigating even if no single prediction looks obviously wrong. The shift itself is the signal.

Latency and error rate are not just an ops concern

A model service that times out under load doesn't fail loudly — it falls back, retries, or gets skipped by a caller that doesn't wait. Tracking latency and error rate alongside accuracy metrics catches a whole class of failure that never shows up in an offline evaluation, because offline evaluation doesn't have a request queue.

The alert that matters is the one tied to a decision

Dashboards accumulate metrics faster than anyone can act on them. The monitoring that actually gets used tends to be narrow: a handful of thresholds tied directly to a runbook, rather than a wall of charts nobody checks until something is already broken. Observability earns its keep not by measuring everything, but by measuring the few things a team has already agreed are worth waking up for.
